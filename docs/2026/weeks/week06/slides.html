Important Discrete Random Variables (pt 2) [DS 3.1, DS 5] [Lec 6]

Here are some additional important discrete random variables:

  • Bernoulli and Binomial distribution.

  • The Negative Binomial distribution (and the geometric distribution; DS section 5.5).

  • The Poisson distribution (from DS section 5.4)

Make sure to read chapter 5 (“special distributions”) for more practice. Also see the Appendix table of distributions for a more exhaustive list; this will be especially useful once we learn about moments of these distributions.

We briefly describe a few of these important discrete distributions.

Suppose you have an infinite sequence of independent Bernoulli trials (i.e. coin flips) with probability \(p\) of success. You will continue the trials until the \(r\)’th success occurs. (Slot machines, anyone?)

The number \(X\) of the failures before you stop is distributed as a Negative Binomial random variable.

NoteDefinition

Negative Binomial distribution.

A random variable \(X\) has the Negative Binomial distribution with parameters \(0<p<1\) and \(r\) (\(r = 0,1,\cdots\)) if the pmf of \(X\) is as follows: \[f(x \mid r, p)= \begin{cases}\left(\begin{array}{c}r+x-1 \\ x\end{array}\right) p^r(1-p)^x & \text { for } x=0,1,2, \ldots \\ 0 & \text { otherwise }\end{cases}\] We write \(X \sim NB(r, p)\).

TipExample

Defective Parts.. Suppose that a machine produces parts that can be either good or defective. Let \(X_i = 1\) if the \(i\)’th part is defective and \(X_i = 0\) otherwise. Assume that the parts are good or defective independently of each other with \(\mathbb{P}(X_i= 1) = p\) for all \(i\). An inspector observes the parts produced by this machine until she sees four defectives. Let \(X\) be the number of good parts observed by the time that the fourth defective is observed. What is the distribution of \(X\)?

A geometric distribution is the special case where \(r=1\). Say you are waiting for the first day it will rain, where the daily probability of raining is \(p\). Then the total number of days it will take to rain is \(X\).

Next, we will study the Poisson random variable and distribution.

NoteDefinition

Poisson distribution.

A random variable \(X\) has the Poisson distribution with mean parameter \(\lambda>0\) if the pmf of \(X\) is as follows: \[f(x\mid \lambda) = \mathbb{P}(X=x) = \begin{cases} \frac{\lambda^{x} e^{-\lambda}}{x!} & \text{ for } x=0,1,\cdots,\\ 0 & \text{ otherwise.} \end{cases}\] For such a random variable, we write \(X \sim Poiss(\lambda)\).

Q: Why is it called a “mean” parameter?

A Poisson random variable (we also say a Poisson-distributed RV) is apt for describing the number of occurrences of an “arrival” process for which (1) arrivals are independent of each other, and (2) the average time between arrivals stays the same. \(\lambda\) is the expected number of occurrences in a unit amount of time. Classic examples are phone calls, earthquakes, and bus arrivals.

TipExample

Customer arrivals. A store owner believes that customers arrive at her store at a rate of \(4.5\) customers per hour on average. She might want to find the distribution of the actual number \(X\) of customers who will arrive during a particular one-hour period later in the day. \(X \sim Poiss(\lambda = 4.5)\). What is the probability that \(3\) customers will arrive in the next hour?

Next, we are interested not only in the number of customers that arrive in the one-hour period, but also in how many customers arrive in the next hour after that period. Let \(Y\) be the number of customers that arrive in the second hour, and model \(Y\) as another Poisson random variable with mean \(4.5\). What is the distribution of \(X+Y\)?

In order to answer this, we need to learn about sums of Poisson. Poisson random variables have the convenient property of being closed to summation.

ImportantTheorem

Poisson sums.

If the random variables \(X_1, \cdots, X_k\) are independent 1 and if \(X_i\) has the Poisson distribution with mean \(\lambda_i (i=1,\cdots,k)\), then the sum \(X = X_1 +\cdots +X_k\) has the Poisson distribution with mean \(\lambda_1 + \cdots + \lambda_k\).

Proof: We will soon learn about moment generating functions, to prove the general case. Let’s prove the \(k=2\) case.

\[\begin{aligned} P(X+Y=k) &=\sum_{i=0}^k P(X+Y=k, X=i) =\sum_{i=0}^k P(Y=k-i, X=i) =\sum_{i=0}^k P(Y=k-i) P(X=i) \\ &=\sum_{i=0}^k e^{-\lambda_1} \frac{\lambda_1^{k-i}}{(k-i) !} e^{-\lambda_2} \frac{\lambda_2^i}{i !} =e^{-(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)} \frac{1}{k !} \sum_{i=0}^k \frac{k !}{i !(k-i) !} \lambda_1^{k-i} \lambda_2^i =e^{-(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)} \frac{1}{k !} \sum_{i=0}^k\left(\begin{array}{l}k \\ i\end{array}\right) \lambda_1^{k-i} \lambda_2^i \\ &=\frac{(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)^k}{k !} \cdot e^{-(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)} \end{aligned}\]

TipExample

Arrivals in two hours instead of one.

Now, returning to the store example, we would also say that \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent because we have been assuming that arrivals (in disjoint time intervals) are independent. Then, we now know that \(Z=X + Y\) would have the Poisson distribution with mean \(4.5 + 4.5 = 9\). What is the probability that at least \(12\) customers will arrive in the entire two-hour period?

We can compute this using probabilities \(\mathbb{P}(Z \ge 12) = 0.1970\) easily using a computer. (In R, the command is 1-ppois(11, 9), which calculates \(1-\mathbb{P}(Z \le 11)\).)

(If we want to be old school, we can go to DS book’s “Table of Poisson Probabilities” and see the column with the parameter \(\lambda = 9\).)

Poisson approximation to the Binomial [DS 3.1, DS 5] [Lec 6]

(This section is meant for self-study.)

TipExample

Customer Arrivals..

A store owner believes that customers arrive at his store at a rate of \(4.5\) customers per hour on average, and wants to find the distribution of the actual number \(X\) of customers who will arrive during a particular one-hour period later in the day.

She models customer arrivals in different time periods as independent of each other. As a first approximation, she divides the one-hour period into \(3600\) seconds and thinks of the arrival rate as being \(4.5/3600= 0.00125\) per second.

She then says that during each second either 0 or 1 customers will arrive, and the probability of an arrival during any single second is \(0.00125\), using the binomial distribution with parameters \(n = 3600\) and \(p= 0.00125\) for the distribution of the number of customers who arrive during the one-hour period later in the day.

She starts calculating \(f\), the pmf of this binomial distribution, and quickly discovers how cumbersome the calculations are. However, she realizes that the successive values of \(f(x)\) are closely related to each other because \(f(x)\) changes in a systematic way as \(x\) increases. So she computes

\[\frac{f(x+1)}{f(x)}=\frac{\binom{n}{x+1} p^{x+1}(1-p)^{n-x-1}}{\binom{n}{x} p^x(1-p)^{n-x}}=\frac{(n-x) p}{(x+1)(1-p)} \approx \frac{n p}{x+1},\]

where the reasoning for the approximation at the end is as follows: For the first 30 or so values of \(x, n-x\) is essentially the same as \(n\) and dividing by \(1-p\) has almost no effect because \(p\) is so small. For example, for \(x=30\), the actual value is \(0.1441\), while the approximation is \(0.1452\). This approximation suggests defining \(\lambda=n p\) and approximating \(f(x+1) \approx f(x) \lambda /(x+1)\) for all the values of \(x\) that matter. That is, \[\begin{aligned} f(1) & =f(0) \lambda \\ f(2) & =f(1) \frac{\lambda}{2}=f(0) \frac{\lambda^2}{2} \\ f(3) & =f(2) \frac{\lambda}{3}=f(0) \frac{\lambda^3}{6} \\ & \vdots \end{aligned}\] Continuing the pattern for all \(x\) yields \(f(x)=f(0) \lambda^x / x!\) for all \(x\).

To obtain a pmf for \(X\), she would need to make sure that \(\sum_{x=0}^{\infty} f(x)=1\). This is easily achieved by setting

\[f(0)=\frac{1}{\sum_{x=0}^{\infty} \lambda^x / x!}=e^{-\lambda}\]

where the last equality follows from the following well-known calculus result:

\[e^\lambda=\sum_{x=0}^{\infty} \frac{\lambda^x}{x!},\]

for all \(\lambda>0\). Hence, \(f(x)=e^{-\lambda} \lambda^x / x!\) for \(x=0,1, \ldots\) and \(f(x)=0\) otherwise is a valid pmf.

This approximation formula for the pmf of a binomial distribution at the end of this example is actually the Poisson pmf that can model many phenomena of types similar to the arrivals of customers. We will state this connection formally.

ImportantTheorem

Poisson Approximation to Binomial Distributions.

When the value of \(n\) is large and the value of \(p\) is close to 0, the binomial distribution with parameters \(n\) and \(p\) can be approximated by the Poisson distribution with mean \(np\).

We will formally prove this in the homework. Let’s see how accurate the approximation is:

Conditional Probability [DS 2.1] [Lec 4]

When you have multiple random phenoma, they rarely have nothing to do with each other. They are often dependent on a common factor, or one is affecting the other, directly or indirectly.

Say a random person is selected from the population; compare the following scenarios:

  • COVID infection rate

  • COVID infection rate if the person is fully vaccinated.

The two probabilities should clearly be different. When more information (“vaccinated”) is available, the probability calculation should reflect it.

NoteDefinition

Conditional probability.

The probability of event \(A\) given event \(B\) has occurred is denoted as \(\mathbb{P}(A|B)\). If \(\mathbb{P}(B)>0\), \[\mathbb{P}(A|B) = \frac{\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)}{\mathbb{P}(B)}.\]

We can interpretate \(\mathbb{P}(A)\) as the “prior” probability of \(A\) without any other information. In contrast, we interpretate \(\mathbb{P}(A|B)\) as the “posterior” probability of \(A\) given \(B\) has occurred.

Let’s try this out on an example.

TipExample

By using a certain prescription drug, there is a 10% chance of experiencing headaches, a 15% chance of experiencing nausea, and a 5% chance of experiencing both headaches and nausea. What is the probability that you will experience an headache given that you feel nauseous after taking the prescription drug?

Note, the conditional probability \(\mathbb{P}(\cdot|B)\) with respect to an event B (with \(\mathbb{P}(B) > 0\)) is a proper probability measure, therefore the three axioms of probability that we discussed in Lecture hold for \(\mathbb{P}(\cdot|B)\) as well. When it comes to standard computations, \(\mathbb{P}(\cdot|B)\) has the same properties as \(\mathbb{P}(\cdot)\). For instance, for disjoint events \(A_1,A_2 \in \Omega\) we have \[P(A_1 \cup A_2|B) = P(A_1|B) + P(A_2|B).\] Neat!

Next, we can relate the joint probability \(\mathbb{P}(A \cap B)\) to event \(B\) using the following rule.

ImportantLemma

Multiplicative rule.

Let \(A,B\) be two events. If \(\mathbb{P}(B)>0\), \[\mathbb{P}(A\cap B) = \mathbb{P}(A|B) {\mathbb{P}(B)}.\] Further, let \(A_i\) be a sequence of events such that \(\mathbb{P}(\cap_{i=1}^{n-1}A_i) >0\). Then, \[\mathbb{P}(\cap_{i=1}^{n} A_i) = \mathbb{P}(A_1)\mathbb{P}(A_2|A_1) \mathbb{P}(A_3|A_1\cap A_2)...\mathbb{P}(A_n|\cap_{i=1}^{n-1}A_i) .\]

Proof. Proof. Proof by induction. First verify the case \(n=2\) holds. Then suppose the case \(n-1\) holds, and show the case \(n\) also holds. ◻

Next, the Bayes rule is about the reversal of the two events \(B|A\); how does it relate to \(A|B\)? Let’s see this next.

ImportantTheorem

Bayes rule.

Let \(A,B\) be two events. If \(\mathbb{P}(B)>0\), \[\mathbb{P}(A|B) = \frac{\mathbb{P}(B|A)\mathbb{P}(A)}{\mathbb{P}(B)},\] where the denominator \(\mathbb{P}(B)=\mathbb{P}(B|A)\mathbb{P}(A)+\mathbb{P}(B|A^c)\mathbb{P}(A^c)\).

Proof. Proof. By definition of the conditional probability and the multiplicative rule. ◻

We will revisit the Bayes rule more in detail in the next section.

The law of total probability allows us to extend the Bayes rule to multiple events \(B_1, \cdots, B_k\) so we can relate \(B_j|A\) to \(A | B_j\).

ImportantTheorem

The law of total probability.

Suppose events \(\{B_j \}\) form a partition of the sample space \(\Omega\) and \(\mathbb{P}(B_j)>0\) for all \(j=1,...,k\). Then, for every event \(A \subset \Omega\), \[\mathbb{P}(A) = \sum\nolimits_{j=1}^k\mathbb{P}(B_j) \mathbb{P}(A|B_j).\]

Proof. Proof. By the definition of partition and the multiplicative rule. ◻

There is a “conditional” version of the law of total probability, which simply uses \(\mathbb{P}(\cdot)\) with \(\mathbb{P}(\cdot | C)\) for some other event \(C\): \[\mathbb{P}(A|C) = \sum\nolimits_{j=1}^k\mathbb{P}(B_j|C) \mathbb{P}(A|B_j\cap C).\]

TipExample

Rare disease.

We are dealing with a rare disease with an overall disease rate: \(\mathbb{P}(D) = 1\%\) where \(D\) is the event that a person has the disease. There is a diagnostic test whose accuracy 95%. Denoting as \(T\) the event that the test is positive, we also know that \(\mathbb{P}(T|D) = 0.95\). We also know that \(\mathbb{P}(T|D^C) = 0.1\). (What are these two events, in words?)

  • Suppose John Doe is diagnosed with positive. What is the probability that he has the disease?

  • Suppose John Doe is diagnosed with negative. What is the probability that he does not have the disease?

  • Suppose John Doe is diagnosed with negative. What is the probability that he does have the disease?

Monty hall.

Bayes rule revisited [DS 2.3] [Lec 4]

(This section is meant for self-study. Also try the examples and practice problems in DS section 2.3.)

Knowing conditional probability, we can derive a useful theorem to calculate the reversed conditional probabilities. Say we have disjoint events \(B_1, \cdots, B_k\). Also take another event \(A\). If \(\mathbb{P}(A|B_i)\) is available for each \(i\), then Bayes theorem is a useful formula for computing the conditional probabilities of the events \(B_i\) given \(A\).

ImportantTheorem

Bayes’ rule.

Let the events \(B_1, \ldots, B_k\) form a partition of the space \(S\) such that \(\mathbb{P}\left(B_j\right)>0\) for \(j=1, \ldots, k\), and let \(A\) be an event such that \(\mathbb{P}(A)>0\). Then, for \(i=1, \ldots, k\), \[\mathbb{P}\left(B_i \mid A\right)=\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(B_i\right) \mathbb{P}\left(A \mid B_i\right)}{\sum_{j=1}^k \mathbb{P}\left(B_j\right) \mathbb{P}\left(A \mid B_j\right)} .\]

Let us prove this. By the definition of conditional probability, \[\mathbb{P}\left(B_i \mid A\right)=\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(B_i \cap A\right)}{\mathbb{P}(A)} .\] The numerator is equal to \(\mathbb{P}\left(B_i \cap A\right)\) by the Multiplicative rule. Similarly, the denominator is equal to \(\mathbb{P}(A)\), which is the sum of the probabilities of the partitions \(B_i \cap A, i = 1,\cdots, k\).

TipExample

Test for a Disease..

Suppose that you are walking down the street and notice that the Department of Public Health is giving a free medical test for a certain disease. The test is 90 percent reliable in the following sense: If a person has the disease, there is a probability of 0.9 that the test will give a positive response; whereas, if a person does not have the disease, there is a probability of only 0.1 that the test will give a positive response. Data indicate that your chances of having the disease are only 1 in 10,000. However, since the test costs you nothing, and is fast and harmless, you decide to stop and take the test. A few days later you learn that you had a positive response to the test. Now, what is the probability that you have the disease?

Side note: The underlined question is a prototype of the question for which Bayes’ rule was designed. We have at least two disjoint events (“you have the disease” and “you do not have the disease”) about which we are uncertain, and we learn a piece of information (the result of the test) that tells us something about the uncertain events. Then we need to know how to revise the probabilities of the events in the light of the information we learned.

At a glance, we may feel that this probability should be about 0.9. However, this feeling completely ignores the small probability of 0.0001 that you had the disease before taking the test.

We shall let \(B_1\) denote the event that you have the disease, and let \(B_2\) denote the event that you do not have the disease. The events \(B_1\) and \(B_2\) form a partition. Also, let \(A\) denote the event that the response to the test is positive. The event \(A\) is information we will learn that tells us something about the partition elements.

Then, by Bayes’ theorem,

\[\begin{aligned} \mathbb{P} \left(B_1 \mid A\right) & =\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(A \mid B_1\right) \mathbb{P}\left(B_1\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(A \mid B_1\right) \mathbb{P}\left(B_1\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(A \mid B_2\right) \mathbb{P}\left(B_2\right)} \\ & =\frac{(0.9)(0.0001)}{(0.9)(0.0001)+(0.1)(0.9999)}=0.00090. \end{aligned}\]

Thus, the conditional probability that you have the disease given the test result is approximately only 1 in 1000. Of course, this conditional probability is approximately 9 times as great as the probability was before you were tested, but even the conditional probability is quite small.

Another way to explain this result is as follows: Only one person in every 10,000 actually has the disease, but the test gives a positive response for approximately one person in every 10. Hence, the number of positive responses is approximately 1000 times the number of persons who actually have the disease. In other words, out of every 1000 persons for whom the test gives a positive response, only one person actually has the disease.

This example illustrates not only the use of Bayes’ theorem but also the importance of taking into account all of the information available in a problem.

Independent events [DS 2.2] [Lec 4]

The event \(A\) is said to be independent of event \(B\) if the occurrence of the event B does not alter the chance that the event A happens. Made formally, this is how independence is defined:

NoteDefinition

Two independent events.

Two events \(A\) and \(B\) are independent if \[\mathbb{P}(A \cap B) = \mathbb{P}(A) \mathbb{P}(B).\] Write \(A\perp B\).

For example, imagine flipping two different fair coins. Assume the outcomes (heads and tail) are equally likely: \(A= \{\text{first try = }T\}\), and \(B= \{\text{second try = }T\}\). Then \(A \perp B\); we understand this intuitively.

In fact, assuming that \(P(B) >0\), we easily see that this is equivalent to \[P(A|B) = P(A \cap B)/P(B) = P(A)P(B)/P(B) = P(A),\] i.e. the occurrence of the event B does not alter the chance that the event A happens.

Furthermore, assuming that also \(P(A) > 0\), we see that \(P(A|B) = P(A)\) is equivalent to \(P(B|A) = P(B)\) (independence is a symmetric relation!).

Let’s try an example where we recognize independence from known probabilities of events.

TipExample

Take the following table of probabilities

\(B\) \(B^C\)
\(A\) 0 0.2
\(A^C\) 0.4 0.4

Are the events A and B independent? Justify your answer.

For multiple events, the definition of independence is trickier. First, all events can be independent of each other:

NoteDefinition

(Mutually) Independent.

For multiple events \(A_j\), mutually independence: for any subset \(\{i_1,...,i_\ell\} \subset \{1,...,n\}\), \[\mathbb{P}(A_{i_1} \cap A_{i_2}... \cap A_{i_\ell} ) = \mathbb{P}(A_{i_1}) ...\mathbb{P}(A_{i_\ell}).\]

Also, when only pairs of events are independent of each other, we call this pairwise independence:

NoteDefinition

Pairwise independence.

For multiple events \(A_j\), pairwise independence: for every \(\{i,j\} \subset \{1,...,n\}\), \[\mathbb{P}(A_{i} \cap A_{j}) = \mathbb{P}(A_{i})\mathbb{P}(A_{j}).\]

Of course, pairwise independent is weaker than (mutually) independent.

TipExample

Mutually independent but not pairwise independent.

Suppose a fair coin is tossed twice. Define the following three events:

  • \(A=\{H \text{ on first toss.}\},\)

  • \(B=\{T \text{ on second toss.}\},\)

  • \(C=\{\text{Both tosses are the same.}\},\)

Are the events mutually independent? Pairwise independent? Prove this.

Here are some properties:

  • If \(A\) and \(B\) are independent, then their complements are also independent.

  • \(\Omega\) and \(\emptyset\) are independent with any event.

Try to prove these on your own time!

Warning: Independence and Disjoint are not the same.

Two events being disjoint simply means that they do not share any jointly occurring elements. For instance, in a single coin flip example, \(A= \{H\}\) and \(B=\{T\}\) are disjoint events, but \(A\) gives perfect knowledge of \(B\), as we know that \(P(A|B) = 0\) and \(P(A|B^C) = 1\), so that \(P(A|B) \neq P(A) = 1/2\).

Finite union


Next, we learn about a formula to calculate the probability of the union of any \(n\) events. You have already seen a version of this for two events \(A\) and \(B\), where \(P(A \cup B) = \underline{\hspace{30mm}}\).

ImportantLemma

Finite union.

For every \(n\) events \(A_1,..,A_n\), \(P(\cup_{i=1}^n A_i)\) is \[\sum \nolimits_{i=1}^n\mathbb{P}(A_i ) - \sum \nolimits _{i<j}^n\mathbb{P}(A_i\cap A_j ) + \sum \nolimits _{i<j<k}^n\mathbb{P}(A_i\cap A_j\cap A_k ) +...+ (-1)^{n+1}\mathbb{P}(A_1 \cap ...\cap A_n).\]

Proof. Proof. Proof by induction. ◻

Using this lemma, we can solve the famous collector’s problem.

TipExample

The Collector’s Problem.

Randomly throw \(n\) balls into \(r\) boxes, what is the probability that every box contains at least one ball? Equivalently, sample \(n\) cards with replacement from \(r\) distinct cards, what is the probability of obtaining a complete set? 1

Lastly, we are now equipped to talk about another ‘parallel’ concept to independence.

NoteDefinition

Two conditional independent events.

Conditional on event \(C\) (such that \(P(C) > 0\)), the events \(A\) and \(B\) are independent if \[\mathbb{P}(A \cap B |C) = \mathbb{P}(A|C) \mathbb{P}(B|C).\] We will write this as \(A \perp B | C\).

This implies that \(P(A | B \cap C) = P(A | C)\). We will prove this in our homework.

Here is an example using conditional independence.

TipExample

Two-stage coin flip experiment.

There is a jar of coins, which are manufactured poorly and have wildly different probabilities of getting a heads. We will draw a coint from the jar, then flip that same coin twice.

Let the event \(C\) be \(\{\text{the coin is biased, such that } \mathbb{P}(\text{Tail in one draw}) = 1/3\}\). What is the probability you will see two heads given that the coin is biased?

Conditional independence does not imply nor is it implied by independence.

Independence does not imply conditional independence. Toss two coins. Let \(H_1\) the even that the first toss is H and \(H_2\) the event that the second toss is \(H\). Let \(D\) the even that the two tosses are different. \(H_1\) and \(H_2\) are clearly independent but \(P\left(H_1 \mid D\right)=P\left(H_2 \mid D\right)=1 / 2\) and \(P\left(H_1 \cap H_2 \mid D\right)=0\).

Conditional independence does not imply independence. We have two coins, a blue one and a red one. For the blue coin the probability of \(H\) is 0.99 and for the red coin it is 0.01 . A coin is chosen at random and then tossed twice. Let \(B\) the event that the blue coin is selected, \(R\) the event that the red coin is selected. Let \(H_1\) and \(H_2\) the events that the first and second toss is \(H\). By the law of total probability

\[P\left(H_1\right)=P\left(H_1 \mid B\right) P(B)+P\left(H_1 \mid R\right) P(R)=1 / 2 .\]

Similarly \(P\left(H_2\right)=1 / 2\). But, using again the law of total probability,

\[P\left(H_1 \cap H_2\right)=P\left(H_1 \cap H_2 \mid B\right) P(B)+P\left(H_1 \cap H_2 \mid R\right) P(R)=1 / 2 \cdot 0.99^2+1 / 2 \cdot 0.01^2 \approx 1 / 2\]

which is different than \(P\left(H_1\right) P\left(H_2\right)=1 / 4\).

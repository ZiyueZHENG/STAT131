Lecture 3: Counting methods

Author

Dr. HYUN

Counting methods [DS 1.7, 1.8] [Lec 3]

It’s not always the case that outcomes of an experiment are equally likely.

However, when they are, we find it very useful to learn how to count the number of outcomes in the event, or in the sample space. Many classical probability calculations can be done by these methods.

Basic techniques from combinatorial analysis come in handy for this type of question. Let’s take a closer look to some relevant cases.

Suppose you have a group of \(m\) elements \(a_1, \cdots ,a_m\) and another group of \(n\) elements \(b_1, \cdots, b_n\). You can form \(mn\) pairs containing one element from each group. (Of course you can easily extend this reasoning to more than just two groups.) This is a useful fact when we are sampling with replacement and the order matters. Let’s make this formal.

ImportantTheorem

The multiplication rule.

Experiment with multiple stages, \(k\) stages; in stage \(i\), there are \(n_i\) options. In total, the number of options is \[\prod \nolimits_{i=1}^k n_i.\]

TipExample

What is the total number of outcomes?

  • rolling two dice

  • toss one coin and roll one die;

  • 10-digit phone numbers

Sampling with and without replacement

The total number of relevant outcomes may heavily depend on whether, in each of your \(k\) stages of your experiment, the items that have already been chosen so far can still be chosen.

NoteDefinition

Sampling with replacement.

Selecting/obtaining \(k\) items from a total of \(n\) items with replacement.

Let’s start with the simpler case of \(k=1\), i.e., selecting one item out of \(n\).

Suppose you toss a six-sided die twice. You have \(6\) simple outcomes on the first roll and \(6\) possible outcomes in the second roll.

In this type of problem, there are a few things to check.

1. Is the experiment performed with replacement? Yes; if the first roll is a \(2\), nothing precludes the fact that the second roll is a \(2\) again.

2. Does the order matter? Yes; the pair \((2,5)\) corresponding to a \(2\) on the first roll and a \(5\) on the second roll is not equal to the pair \((5,2)\) corresponding to a \(5\) on the first roll and a \(2\) on the second roll. We will revisit this when we learn about permutations.

TipExample

Suppose a box contains \(6\) numbered balls; for each selection, record the number and put the selected ball back to the box. Is this sampling with or without replacement? What is the total number of possible outcomes?

(Note, the experiment where you roll two fair dice is the same experiment as one dice being independently rolled twice.)

Next, what if the sampling is done without replacement?

NoteDefinition

Sampling without replacement.

Selecting \(k\) items from a total of \(n\) items without replacement.

Here is an example. We’d like to count how many different subsets of three people can become officers of an organization of \(n=30\) people, if chosen randomly? Order doesn’t matter here (we are not interested in the exact appointments for a given candidate). The random sampling of random three officers from \(n\) people is a sampling conducted without replacement.

Here are some other examples:

  1. Texas Holdem: 3 common cards are dealt sequentially from a deck of cards. 2 cards are dealt to each player from the same deck.

  2. Blackjack: 2 cards are dealt to each player from the same pile of cards.

  3. Lotto numbers are drawn sequentially.

Permutation, Combinations, and Partitions

We will learn three tools for counting. This is called combinatorics, and is an elegant branch of mathematics that we borrow from.

  1. Permutations

  2. Combinations

  3. Partitions

First, we use a core counting operation called factorials.

NoteDefinition

Factorial.

Factorial \(n!\) is defined as \[n! =n\times (n-1) \times ... \times 2 \times 1.\]

A factorial is useful for counting permutations.

NoteDefinition

All permutations.

The number of distinct orderings of \(n\) elements is \(n!\).

Next, we define permutation number \(P_{n,k}\), the way to order \(k\) elements chosen from \(n\) elements; also called \(k\)-permutation.

NoteDefinition

Permutation number.

Permutation number \(P_{n,k}\) is the number of distinct orderings such that \(k\) elements are selected from \(n\) items in order, without replacement. \[P_{n,k} = n!/(n-k)! = n \cdot (n-1) \cdot (n-2), \cdots, (n-r+1).\] Note: Order matters!

Consider the example from before. There are 30 members in a student organization and they need to choose a president, a vice-president, and a treasurer. In how many ways can this be done? The size of the \(n\) distinct elements (persons) is \(n = 30\), the number of persons to be appointed is \(r = 3\). The sampling is done without replacement (a president is chosen out of \(30\) people, then among the \(29\) people left a vice-president is chosen, and finally among the \(28\) people left a treasurer is chosen).

Does the order matter? Yes, the president is elected first, then the vice-president, and finally the treasurer (think about ‘ranking’ the 30 candidates). The number of ways in which the three positions can be assigned is therefore \(P_{30,3} = 30!/(30-3)! = 30 \cdot 29 \cdot 28 = 24360.\)

Note, the number of ways to choose \(n\) out of \(n\) elements is \(P_{n,n}\) simplifies to \(n\) factorial (\(n!\)). In this case, no choosing occurs; we only need to count the ways to order.

TipExample

Choosing officers.

Suppose a club has 25 members, a president and a secretary are to be chosen from the membership. How many distinct configurations can we have?

TipExample

Bookshelf.

Suppose you have 10 books on your bookshelf, how many ways are there to arrange all of the books?

TipExample

Birthday problem.

What is the probability that at least two of the students in STAT131 have the same birthday?

Hint: \(E=\{\)at least two of the students have the same birthday\(\}\) and we are interested in \(\mathbb{P}(E)\).

Approximate factorials Most computers cannot handle \(n!\) and arithmetic operations when \(n\) is big. The trick is to complete the arithmetic operations in log-scale and exponentiate the results in the end.

To compute \(\frac{P_{n,k}}{n^k}\), we compute \(\log(P_{n,k}) - k \log (n)\) where \(\log(P_{n,k}) = \log(n!)-\log((n-k)!) = \sum_{i=n-k+1}^n\log(i)\). Identity: \(\frac{P_{n,k}}{n^k} = e^{\log(P_{n,k}) - k \log (n) }\) where \(\log(\cdot)\) is the natural log.

NoteDefinition

Combination number.

Suppose a set has \(n\) elements, select \(k\) elements without replacement. Each subset of size \(k\) is a combination of \(n\) elements taken \(k\) at a time. The number of distinct combinations: \[C_{n,k} = \frac{P_{n,k}}{k!} = \frac{n!} {(n-k)!k!}.\] Also known as binomial coefficients \(\binom{n}{k}\). Note: Order doesn’t matter!

The combination number is used in the famed binomial theorem.

ImportantTheorem

Binomial theorem.

\[(x+y)^n = \sum_{i=0}^n \binom{n}{k} x^ky^{n-k}.\]

TipExample

Complete square: \((x+y)^2= ?\) and \((x+y)^3= ?\).

ImportantLemma

A few frequently used properties of binomial coefficients:

  • \(\binom{n}{0} = \binom{n}{n} =1\),

  • \(\binom{n}{k} = \binom{n}{n-k}\).

  • \(\binom{n}{k} + \binom{n}{k-1} = \binom{n+1}{k}\).

  • Vandermonde’s identity: \[\binom{m+n}{k} = \sum_{j=0}^k\binom{m}{j} \binom{n}{k-j}.\]

TipExample

Texas Holdem.

A 5-card hand is dealt from a standard, well-shuffled 52-card deck. The hand is called a full house in poker if it consists of three cards of some rank and two cards of another rank. e.g. three \(K\)’s + two 7’s, where ordering does not matter. What is the probability of a full house?

TipExample

Blood Alleles.

The gene for human blood type consists of a pair of alleles chosen from the three alleles commonly called O, A, and B. For example, two possible combinations of alleles (called genotypes) to form a blood-type gene would be BB and AO. We will not distinguish the same two alleles in different orders, so OA represents the same genotype as AO. How many genotypes are there for blood type?

TipExample

Fair coin tossed.

A fair coin is to be tossed 10 times. Determine the probability that exactly 3 heads will occur? Also determine the probability that three or fewer heads will occur?

TipExample

Sampling without replacement.

Suppose an urn contains 15 yellow and 30 blue balls, from which 10 balls will be selected at random. What is the probability that exactly three yellow balls will be selected?

Next, suppose we want to partition a set of \(n\) elements into \(r\) subsets so that the \(i\)’th subset contains \(n_i\) elements, and \(\sum_i n_i = n\). How many ways to do this?

NoteDefinition

Partitions.

The number of ways to partition \(n\) elements into \(n_1, \cdots, n_r\) groups is \[\binom{n}{n_1,\cdots, n_r}.\] This is the multinomial coefficient.

Q: Can you see how the combinations \(C_{n,k}\) are a specific case of partitions, so that the binomial coefficient is a specific case of the multinomial coefficient?

TipExample

How many different words (even meaningless) can be obtained by rearranging the word PEPPER?

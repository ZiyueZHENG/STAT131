Continuous random variables [DS 3.2] [Lec 7]

Definition and Properties

We have so far learned about discrete random variables.

But many random variables can take values in certain intervals. For instance, human height/weight measurements, temperature, precipitation, etc. We model these using continuous random variables.

NoteDefinition

continuous random variable.

The random variable \(X\) is continuous, if \(\exists\) a function \(f: \bR \to \R_+\) such that \[P(a \le X\le b) = \int_{a}^bf(x)dx .\] Probability density function (pdf): the above \(f\).

How is a pdf related to events’ probabilities? For continuous random variable \(X\), the probability of event \(E\) is \[\mathbb{P}(E) = \int \nolimits_{x\in E} f(x) dx.\]

It should be noted that a probability density function (pdf) is not the same as probability. For example, for a continuous random variable \(X\), \(P(X=a) =0\) but its pdf at \(a\) can be strictly positive.

NoteDefinition

Support.

Support of continuous random variable \(X\) is defined as the (closure of) \(\{x: f(x) >0\}\) where \(f(x)\) is the pdf.

It is also useful to note:

  • \(f(x) \ge 0\) for all \(x \in \bR\), and

  • \(\int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x)dx =1\).

(Note, it naturally follows that \(\int_{supp(X)} f(x)dx =1\), since for values of \(x\) outside of the support, \(f(x)=0\).)

Sometimes we only know or recognize the pdf up to a constant (that is, constant with respect to \(x\)): \[f(x) =c(\theta) g_\theta(x),\] where \(g_\theta(x)\) is known, \(\theta\) is some constant (“parameter”). We can write this as \(f(x) \propto g_\theta(x)\).

NoteDefinition

Normalizing constant.

For pdf such that \[f(x) =c(\theta) g_\theta(x),\] \(c(\theta)\) is the normalizing constant.

Without the normalizing constant \(c(\theta)\), the pdf is incomplete. How to compute \(c(\theta)\) then? The idea is to use the property that: \[\int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x)dx =1.\]

(The same idea applies to pmfs, whose probabilities \(\sum_{x \in \text{Supp}(X)} f(x)dx =1\)

TipExample

For continuous random variable \(X\), \(f(x) \propto e^{-2x}\) for \(x>0\); \(f(x)=0\) for \(x\le 0\).

  • Compute the normalizing constant.

  • Compute \(P(1<X<2)\).

In order to fully specify a probability distribution, you must calculate the normalizing constant using the above technique. But often, the \(g_\theta(x)\) (sometimes called a “kernel”) is enough to identify a distribution. In fact, when you see that the kernel of one pdf matches that of another distribution, the two random variables are distributed the same.

TipExample

Identifying a distribution using kernel.

If the random variable \(X\) has probability density function \(f_X(x) = c(n, p) \cdot p^x (1-p)^{n-x}\), what is the distribution of \(X\)?

Just as with discrete random variables, we can define the expectation of a continuous random variable as a probability-weighted average of the possible values.

NoteDefinition

Expectation.

For continuous random variable \(X\), \[\mathbb{E}[X] = \int\nolimits_{-\infty}^\infty xf(x)dx,\] provided \(\mathbb{E}[|X|] < \infty\).

We will put this to use soon.

Important Continuous Random Variables [DS 3.2, DS 5] [Lec 7]

In this section, we will introduce a few important distributions. Here is an extensive list of commonly used continuous random variables.

  • The Uniform distribution

  • Normal (Gaussian) distribution

  • Gamma distribution: special case is exponential (and chi-square.)

  • The \(\chi^2\) (Chi-squared) distribution

  • The Beta distribution: special case is uniform.

  • The student\(-t\) distribution (advanced)

  • Cauchy distribution (advanced)

We will introduce a few of these. Make sure to study the rest in DS section 3.2 and section 5. You will be tested on them.

NoteDefinition

Uniform.

The random variable \(X\) is uniform on \([a,b]\). Its pdf is \[f(x) = \frac{1}{b-a} 1_{x \in [a,b]}.\] We write \(X \sim U(a,b)\) or \(X \sim \text{Uniform}(a,b)\) or \(X \sim \text{Unif}(a,b)\).

TipExample

Expectation of uniform.

1. If random variable \(X\) is distributed as \(U(0,1)\), what is the expected value?

2. If you pick a random uniform value between \(a\) and \(b\), what is the expected value?

Next, the Normal distribution is one of the most relevant distributions for statistical modeling in a wide array of applictions.

NoteDefinition

Normal.

The random variable \(X\) has a Normal distribution with parameters \(\mu \in \mathbb R\) and \(\sigma \in \mathbb R^+\) if its pdf is \[f(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2 \pi \sigma^2}} e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2 \sigma^2}}.\] We write \(X\sim \mathcal{N} (\mu, \sigma^2)\). Also, the Standard Normal distribution is the Normal distribution but with specific parameters \(\mu = 0\) and \(\sigma^2=1\).

(The two parameters \(\mu\) and \(\sigma^2\) are sometimes called the mean and variance of the distribution.)

TipExample

Normal mean and variance.

We can see, from examining the pdf, that

1. A Normal random variable is symmetric around its mean \(\mu\),

2. The standard deviation parameter \(\sigma\) (square root of the variance) dictates the spread of the random variable’s potential values. (Use \(\mu = 0\) for simplicity.)

These distributions are the first ones we learn about whose pdf cannot be integrated in closed form. So, we need tables of the cdf values or computer programs in order to compute probabilities and quantiles for normal distributions.

Here are some basic properties of a Normal distribution.

  • A normal pdf is bell-shaped and symmetric around \(\mu\).

  • If \(X\) has the normal distribution with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\) and if \(Y= aX + b\) for constants \(a\neq 0\) and \(b\), then \(Y\) has the normal distribution with mean \(a\mu + b\) and variance \(a^2\sigma^2\). 1

Why is the Normal distribution so important? There are three reasons.

The first reason is directly related to the mathematical properties of the normal distributions. If a random samples (that is, many independent repeated random variables) are from a normal distribution, then the distributions of various important functions of the observations in the sample can be derived explicitly and have simple forms. So, it is mathematically convenient to be able to assume measurements are drawn from a normal distribution.

TipExample

Sample mean and sample variance.

Let’s say we have \(n\) random independent random variables all distributed as \(\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\). Call them \(X_1,X_2, \cdots, X_n\).

1. The Sample Mean (\(\bar{X} = \frac{\sum_i X_i}{n}\)) of independent normal variables is itself normally distributed. (This is because any linear combination of independent normal variables is normal.) \[\bar{X} \sim N(\mu, \frac{\sigma^2}{n})\]

2. The Sample Variance (\(S^2\)): While the sample mean follows a Normal distribution, the sum of squared deviations follows a Chi-square (\(\chi^2\)) distribution, which we will learn about later in this lecture. Specifically, the ratio of the “sample variance”: \[S^2 = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n(X - \bar X)^2}{n-1}\] to the true variance \(\sigma^2\) (scaled by so-called “degrees of freedom” \(n-1\)) follows this form: \[\frac{(n-1)S^2}{\sigma^2} \sim \chi^2_{n-1}.\]

The second reason is that many scientists have observed that the random variables studied in various physical experiments often have distributions that are approximately normal. For example, a normal distribution will usually be a close approximation to the distribution of the heights or weights of individuals in a homogeneous population of people, corn stalks, or mice, or to the distribution of the tensile strength of pieces of steel produced by a certain process.

(Sometimes, a simple transformation of the observed random variables has a normal distribution.)

The third reason: if a large random sample (e.g. \(200\) random independent measurements) is taken from some distribution, then even though this distribution is not itself normal, many important functions of the observations (e.g. the average) in the sample will have distributions which are approximately normal. 2

Next, the gamma distribution is a popular choice for modeling random variables that are known to be positive.

NoteDefinition

Gamma Distribution..

A random variable \(X\) has the gamma distribution with parameters \(\alpha>0\) and \(\beta>0\) if \(X\) has a continuous distribution for which the pdf is \[f(x \mid \alpha, \beta)= \begin{cases}\frac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)} x^{\alpha-1} e^{-\beta x} & \text { for } x>0 \\ 0 & \text { for } x \leq 0\end{cases}\]

Here, the gamma function \(\Gamma(\cdot)\) is (for \(\alpha >0\)): \[\Gamma(\alpha) = \int_0^\infty x^{\alpha-1} e^{-x} \;dx,\] and has these properties

  • \(\Gamma(1)=\int_0^\infty e^{-x} dx = 1\)

  • \(\Gamma(\alpha) = (\alpha - 1) \Gamma(\alpha-1)\) for \(\alpha > 1\).

  • \(\Gamma(n) = (n-1)!\) for any positive integer \(n\).

Gamma random variables add up to also be gamma distributed. That is, if \(X_1,\cdots, X_n\) are independent gamma random variables3, and each \(X_i\) is gamma distributed with parameters \(\alpha_i\) and \(\beta\), then the sum \[X_1+\cdots+ X_n\] also has a Gamma distribution with parameters \(\sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i\) and \(\beta\).

Next, a special case of gamma distributions provide a common model for phenomena such as waiting times.

NoteDefinition

Exponential distribution.

A random variable \(X\) has the Exponential distribution with parameters \(\beta >0\) if the pdf of \(X\) is as follows: \[f(x \mid \beta)= \begin{cases} \beta e^{-\beta x} & \text{for } x > 0,\\ 0 & \text{for } x \le 0.\end{cases}\] For such a random variable, we write \(X \sim Exp(\beta)\).

Q: Do you see the exponential pdf can be written succinctly as \(f(x \mid \beta)=\beta e^{-\beta x} \mathbbm{1}_{\{x > 0\}}\)?

If the wait time (in hours) for a bus is modeled as a random variable with distribution \(Exp(1/2)\), this means that the expected value of the wait time is \(30\) minutes. Q: Can you see why?

4

The exponential distribution is the only distribution that is memoryless, which means \[\mathbb{P}(X \ge t+h| X\ge t) = \mathbb{P}(X \ge h).\]

Let’s briefly prove this. For each \(t>0\), \[\mathbb{P}(X \geq t)=\int_t^{\infty} \beta e^{-\beta x} d x=e^{-\beta t}\]

Hence, for each \(t>0\) and each \(h>0\), \[\begin{aligned} \mathbb{P} (X \geq t+h \mid X \geq t) & =\frac{\mathbb{P}(X \geq t+h)}{\mathbb{P}(X \geq t)} \\ & =\frac{e^{-\beta(t+h)}}{e^{-\beta t}}=e^{-\beta h}=\mathbb{P}(X \geq h). \end{aligned}\]

To illustrate the memoryless property, let’s suppose that \(X\) represents the number of minutes5 that elapse before some event occurs. If the event has not occurred during the first \(t\) minutes, then the probability that the event will not occur during the next \(h\) minutes is simply \(e^{-\beta h}\). This is the same as the probability that the event would not occur during an interval of \(h\) minutes starting from time \(0\).

In other words, regardless of the length of time that has elapsed without the occurrence of the event, the probability that the event will occur during the next \(h\) minutes always remains the same.

Here is one application of this, which you’ll also see in your homework!

TipExample

Memoryless wait times.

Suppose that an exam is to be taken by two students independently of one another, and the number of minutes required by each student to complete the exam has the exponential distribution with parameter \(1/40\).

Let’s say the first student finishes at 9:20 a.m. What is the probability probability that the other student will complete the exam before 10:00 a.m.?

(Hint: once the first student finishes, the other student are still taking the test. What does the memoryless property say here?)

Also, suppose that the variables \(X_1,\cdots, X_n\) are independent exponential random variables with parameter \(\beta\). Then, the distribution of: \[Y_{(1)} = \min\{X_1, . . . , X_n\},\] will be the exponential distribution with parameter \(n \beta\).

TipExample

Say there are three bus lines will take you to your lecture hall from the entrance of the campus. The wait time for any of the bus lines is well modeled by an exponential random variable with parameter \(1/20\) (which means it will take on average \(20\) minutes for that bus to arrive). What is the distribution of the time it will take to get on the next bus that will take you to class?

Next, we introduce and discuss a particular class of gamma distributions known as the chi-square (\(\chi^2\)) distributions. These distributions, which are closely related to random samples from a normal distribution, are widely applied in the field of statistics. (In STAT132, you will see how they are applied in important problems of statistical inference!)

For each positive number \(m\), the gamma distribution with parameters \(\alpha=m/2\) and \(\beta=1/2\) is called the \(\chi^2\) distribution with \(m\) degrees of freedom.

NoteDefinition

Chi-square \(\chi^2_m\) distribution distribution.

For each positive number \(m\), the gamma distribution with parameters \(\alpha=m/2\) and \(\beta=1/2\) is called the \(\chi^2\) distribution with \(m\) degrees of freedom.

The pdf of a random variable \(X\) that has the \(\chi^2\) distribution with \(m\) degrees of freedom is (for \(x>0\)): \[f(x)=\frac{1}{2^{m / 2} \Gamma(m / 2)} x^{(m / 2)-1} e^{-x / 2}.\] (\(f(x)=0\) for \(x \leq 0\).)

The parameter \(m\) is called “degrees of freedom” because a chi square distribution can be defined also as the sum of \(m\) squared standard normal random variables.

If the random variables \(X_1, \ldots, X_m\) are independent and if \(X_i\) has the standard normal (\(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\)) distribution, then the sum \(X_1^2+\cdots+X_m^2\) has the \(\chi^2\) distribution with \(m\) degrees of freedom (or in short, a \(\chi_m^2\) distribution.)

Also, more generally, if the random variables \(X_1, \ldots, X_k\) are independent and if \(X_i\) has the \(\chi^2\) distribution with \(m_i\) degrees of freedom ( \(i=1, \ldots, k\) ), then the sum \(X_1+\cdots+X_k\) has the \(\chi^2\) distribution with \(m_1+\cdots+m_k\) degrees of freedom.

NoteDefinition

Beta distribution.

A random variable \(X\) has the Beta distribution with parameters \(\alpha, \beta >0\) if the pdf of \(X\) is as follows: \[f(x \mid \alpha, \beta)= \frac{x^{\alpha-1}(1-x)^{\beta-1}}{B(\alpha, \beta)} \mathbbm{1}_{\{x \in [0,1]\}}\] where \[B(\alpha, \beta)=\int_0^1 x^{\alpha-1}(1-x)^{\beta-1} d x=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha) \Gamma(\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}\]

Q: For what values of \(\alpha\), \(\beta\) do you get a Uniform distribution \(U(0,1)\) as a special case?

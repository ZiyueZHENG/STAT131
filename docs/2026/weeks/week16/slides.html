Lec 16: Convergence of Random variables

Author

Dr. HYUN

Modes of Convergence [DS 6.2-6.3, AOS 6.1-6.3] [Lec 16]

Sure convergence or pointwise convergence.

So far, the number of the random variables has been fixed as an integer \(n\in \N_+\). But often, we would like to know what happens at the limit; when \(n\to\infty\). Imagine a sequence of random variables \(X_1, X_2, \cdots\). Do they converge, and in what sense?

NoteDefinition

Convergence in probability.

\(X_n\) converges to \(b\) in probability, written \(X_n \ipto X\), if for every \(\epsilon>0\), \[\mathbb{P}(|X_n-b| >\epsilon) \to 0,\] as \(n\to \infty\).

TipExample

Show the following by definition.

  • If a sequence of random variables \(X_n\) are independently distributed as \(\text{Bernoulli}(\frac{1}{n})\), show that \(X_n \ipto 0\).

  • If \(X_n\) is distributed as \(N(\mu, \sigma^2/n)\), show that \(X_n\ipto \mu\).

Almost sure convergence.

Shrinking Bernoulli’s.

NoteDefinition

Convergence in distribution.

\(X_n\) converges to \(X\) in distribution, written \(X_n \idto X\), if \[\lim_{n\to \infty} F_n(t) = F(t)\] at all \(t\) for which \(F\) is continuous. The distribution on the right is called the limiting distribution.

TipExample

Show the following: If \(X_n\) are distributed as \(Bin(n, \lambda/n)\) (for \(\lambda>0\)), then \(X_n \idto \text{Poisson}(\lambda)\).

ImportantTheorem

Suppose random variable \(X_i\sim F_i\) with mgf \(\psi_i\), and some random variable \(X^*\) has mgf \(\psi^*\). If \(\psi_i(t) \to \psi^*(t)\) for all \(t\) in some interval around \(t=0\), then \(X_i \idto X^*\).

TipExample

Show the following using moment generating functions: If \(X_n\) are distributed as \(Bin(n, \lambda/n)\) (for \(\lambda>0\)), then \(X_n \idto \text{Poisson}(\lambda)\).

Convergence in quadratic mean.

\(L_p\) convergence.

The Law of Large Numbers [DS 6.2-6.3, AOS 6.1-6.3] [Lec 16]

In various applications, the sample mean, or sample average: \[\bar X_n = \frac{1}{n} \sum\nolimits_{i=1}^n X_i = \frac{X_1 + X_2 + \cdots + X_n}{n}\] is of key importance. We will use \(\bar X\) to denote the average of \(n\) random variables \(X_1,\cdots, X_n\).

What would happen as \(n\to \infty\)? In particular, does \(\bar X_n\) converge as \(n\to \infty\)? If so, (1) in what sense and (2) to what value does \(\bar X_n\) converge to?

We can be more concrete. Let’s say we throw a coin multiple times, and record the numbers as \(X_1, \cdots, X_n\). We can inuit (expect) that the average \((X_1 + X_2 + \cdots + X_n)/n\) will be close to \(0.5\).

The laws of large numbers establishes this rigorously, in a fairly general setting.

Basic properties of sample mean

Before answering the question of \(n\to \infty\), we first examine properties of the sample mean when \(n\) is finite.

  • The mean of sample mean is \[\mathbb{E}[\bar X_n] = \mathbb{E}[X_1],\] (provided that \(\mathbb{E}[X_1]\) exists).

  • The variance of sample mean is \[V(\bar X_n)= \frac{1}{n}V(X_1),\] (provided that \(V(X_1)\) exists.)

Next, we learn our first, celebrated result about how the sample mean probabilistically converges to the true mean.

ImportantTheorem

Weak Law of Large Numbers.

For iid sample \(X_1,...,X_n\) whose mean and variance exist, \[\bar X_n \;\ipto \;\mathbb{E}[X_1].\]

The qualifier “weak” is for convergence in probability – wLLN for short. (There is also a strong law of large numbers, which is beyond the scope of this class.)

Proof. Proof. Recall Chebyshev inequality. For random variable \(X\) (whose variance exists), we have \[\mathbb{P}(|X-\mathbb{E}[X] |>t ) \le V(X)/t^2.\]

Next, for iid \(X_i\)’s, \[\mathbb{P}( |\bar X_n- \mathbb{E}[X_1] | \ge t) \le \frac{V(X_1)}{nt^2}.\]

As \(t>0\) is arbitrary1, we can choose it to be very small. This proves that: \[\bar X_n \ipto \mathbb{E}[X_1].\] ◻

Next, we examine an interesting application of wLLN.

TipExample

Histogram.

A histogram is the result of binning data \(X_1,\cdots, X_n\) and measuring the relative frequency in the bins. The histogram height from a bin between \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) is \[\bar Y_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^n1(X_i\in [c_1,c_2) )\] for some interval \([c_1,c_2)\). \(\bar Y_n\) is the sample mean of iid Bernoulli random variables.

By wLLN, \[\bar Y_n \ipto \mathbb{E}[1(X_1\in [c_1,c_2)] = \mathbb{P}(X_i \in [c_1,c_2)).\]

TipExample

Determine the required sample size.

Suppose \(X_1,...,X_n\distras{iid} F\) whose mean and variance exist, and we have an upper bound on \(V(X_1)\), say \(V_0\), we have \[\mathbb{P}( |\bar X_n- \mathbb{E}[X_1] | \ge t) \le \frac{V_0}{nt^2}.\] In other words, with probability at least \(1- \frac{V_0}{nt^2}\), \[|\bar X_n- \mathbb{E}[X_1] | \le t.\]

Suppose we have a target/tolerable error \(t_0\). We can choose the sample size \(n\) to control the probability bound \[1- \frac{V_0}{nt_0^2}.\]

For instance, \(t_0=0.01\), \(V_0=1\), if we hope to achieve 99% confidence, what sample size \(n\) is needed?

Note, we have required that the variance is finite (\(V[X_i]<\infty\)).

The Central Limit Theorem [DS 6.2-6.3, AOS 6.1-6.3] [Lec 16]

We finally arrive at the central limit theorem (CLT) which provides a simple yet powerful way of approximating probabilities.

ImportantTheorem

Central Limit Theorem.

Take \(X_1,X_2,...,X_n\) that are independent random variables each with mean \(\mathbb{E}[X_1] =\mu\) and \(V[X_1] =\sigma^2\). Then for each fixed number \(t\), \[\lim_{n\to \infty} \mathbb{P}( \sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n -\mu)/\sigma \le t ) = \Phi(t),\] where \(\Phi(t)\) is the cdf of a standard normal (\(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\)) random variable. We write this as \[\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n -\mu)/\sigma \idto N(0,1).\]

Proof. Proof. The mgf of \(\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n -\mu)/\sigma\) with \(X_i\)’s mgf \(\psi\): \[\psi_n(t) = \mathbb{E}[e^{t \sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n -\mu)/\sigma}] = \mathbb{E}[e^ {\frac{t}{\sqrt{n}} \sum_{i=1}^n (\frac{X_i-\mu}{\sigma})} ].\] We would like to use Theorem [thm:mgf-converge]; so, the goal is to show \(\psi_n(t) \to e^{t^2/2}\) for \(t\) in some interval around \(t=0\).

Let \(Y_i =\frac{X_i-\mu}{\sigma}\), so \(Y_i, i=1\cdots,n\) are iid with mean \(0\) and variance \(1\). With the Taylor expansion: \[\psi_Y(t) = \psi_Y(0) + \psi_Y^{(1)}(0) t + \frac{1}{2!} \psi_Y^{(2)}(0) t^2 + \frac{1}{3!} \psi_Y^{(3)}(0) t^3+...,\] it follows that \[\psi_n(t) = \mathbb{E}[e^ {\frac{t}{\sqrt{n}} \sum_{i=1}^n Y_i } ] = [\psi_Y({t}/{\sqrt{n}})]^n \to e^{t^2/2}.\] ◻

A direct implication is that: for \(n\) large enough, \[\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n -\mu)/\sigma \approx N(0,1).\] which means that the “standardized” random variable \(\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n -\mu)/\sigma\) with a large but fixed \(n\) follows the standard Normal distribution.

Since we know very well how to handle Normal random variables and how to calculate probabilities from them, the CLT provides a simple yet powerful way of approximating probabilities.

TipExample

Normal approximation.

In many cases, for \(n\) being a few tens, the approximation is reasonably accurate.

  • Suppose \(X_i \distras{iid} Ber(p)\) for \(i=1,..,n\), and \(n=100\). We can apply the Normal approximation to the following probability \[\mathbb{P}(\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^n X_i \ge 70).\]

  • Suppose \(X_i \distras{iid} Pois(\lambda)\) for \(i=1,..,n\), and \(n=100\). We can apply the Normal approximation to the following probability \[\mathbb{P}( |\bar{X}_n-\lambda|<c).\]

  • Suppose \(X_i \distras{iid} Exp(\lambda)\) for \(i=1,..,n\), and \(n=100\). We can apply the Normal approximation to the following probability \[\mathbb{P}( |\bar{X}_n- \lambda|<c).\]

TipExample

Confidence interval.

Find a confidence interval for the average of 50 test scores.

TipExample

Suppose that the number of errors per computer program has a Poisson distribution with mean 5. There are 125 programs created by 125 programmers. Let \(X_1, \dots , X_{125}\) be the number of errors in the programs. What is the probability of the average number of errors being not so high, say within 0.5 of the mean 5?

TipExample

Determine the sample size.

Suppose \(X_i \distras{iid} Pois(\lambda)\) for \(i=1,..,n\). Determine \(n\) such that \[\mathbb{P}( |\bar{X}_n-\lambda|< 0.1 ) > 0.95.\] Alternatively, the Chebyshev inequality can also be used to provide an answer. Which one is more accurate?
